# SECOND CURSOR: uncoached playtest

## Purpose

Find out whether the cursor confrontation is understandable and worth sharing without an explanation from its creator. This is a qualitative test, not evidence of market demand or a prediction of virality.

## Setup

Recruit five people who have not seen the game, including some who play short horror games. Test individually, with a physical mouse and headphones at a comfortable volume. Tell them the game contains sudden horror effects. Ask permission before recording screen, voice or face. Do not publish recordings without separate consent.

Use the current Windows full build on Normal with default timing. Use a new isolated save directory for each participant, passed through `-scsavedir`; never erase someone's personal save. Allow accessibility settings and record their choices. Avoid debug jumps, instructions from the developer, or time acceleration.

Say only: "Play as you normally would. You can stop whenever you want. If something is confusing, say what you are trying to do."

## Observe

- Time to first meaningful work action and first cursor confrontation.
- Where they hesitate, repeat actions, or ask for help. Note what was visible at that moment.
- Whether they can distinguish their cursor from the other cursor.
- Whether they notice a file being protected before text explains it.
- Whether they understand a cancelled shred, a completed shift, and an actual death as different outcomes.
- Whether they retry voluntarily after a capture, and whether repeated work feels tedious.
- Any spontaneous laugh, protest, surprise, or request to show someone a moment. Write down their words and timestamp without labelling the reaction as viral.

If progress stops for two minutes, ask "What are you trying to do?" Give help only if they request it or choose to stop. Record exactly what help you gave; that section no longer counts as uncoached.

## Ask afterwards, in this order

1. Who were you, and what was your job?
2. What did the other cursor want? What made you think that?
3. What happened at the end? What would you do next?
4. Which moment, if any, would you show someone? Why that moment?
5. Would you recommend this to a specific friend? What would you tell them?
6. What price would feel fair for the experience you played?
7. If the finished game cost $7, would you buy it, wait for a sale, or pass? Why?
8. What would make you stop playing or refund it?

Ask the open price question before revealing $7. Do not ask "Was the cursor fight the viral moment?" or praise a desired answer. Purchase intent in an interview is not an actual purchase.

## Decision gate

For this small round, aim for at least four of five players to explain who they are, who controls each cursor, and whether the ending was a loss, without coaching. Aim for at least three to independently choose the cursor confrontation as something they would share. These are internal iteration targets, not statistically validated thresholds.

If people understand it but do not care, improve the confrontation's agency and consequences. If they care but cannot explain what happened, fix the specific cue they missed. If deaths cause quitting, reduce repetition or improve the warning before increasing punishment. Test the revision with new people.
